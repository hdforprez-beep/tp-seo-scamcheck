The Contractor's SEO Scam-Check

Ten things to check before you pay another marketing company. About ten minutes.

It is hard to tell whether your SEO is actually working, and that is not a knock on you.

The work happens where you cannot see it. The report arrives once a month in language nobody explains. And the question you actually care about, whether any of this put a job on the board, is the one it tends to skip.

So here is the checklist. Run it with your last invoice and your phone in front of you. You do not need to know anything about SEO to use it.

How to use it: tap a box for anything that sounds like your setup. Several of these you can settle tonight from your phone. Add them up at the bottom.

Part one

The ten red flags

Why it matters
Nobody owns the algorithm, so nobody can promise a position in it. Google puts this in writing on its own page about hiring an SEO: "No one can guarantee a #1 ranking on Google." The same page tells business owners to "Beware of SEOs that claim to guarantee rankings, allege a 'special relationship' with Google, or advertise a 'priority submit' to Google."
What to do
Ask them to put the guarantee in writing, with the exact keyword and what you get back if it is missed. The answer to that email tells you a lot.

Source: Google Search Central, Do you need an SEO?

Why it matters
Rankings, traffic and impressions are all inputs. What you are actually buying is booked work. If nobody at the agency can point to a single job, then nobody there knows whether the money is working either.
What to do
Ask for one example: the job, the call or form it started with, and the date. They should be able to trace it in their own tracking and reporting.
Why it matters
Impressions do not sign contracts. A report can be full of traffic and keyword positions and still never connect that activity to a customer. The pattern worth noticing is a graph going up while the phone stays quiet. Google's own guidance says it plainly: "Be careful if a company is secretive or won't clearly explain what they intend to do."
What to do
Ask for next month's report to open with three lines: calls, form fills, and booked work from unpaid Google results. If that is a problem, ask why.

Source: Google Search Central, Do you need an SEO?

Why it matters
A ranking promise gets much easier when the target is a phrase almost nobody searches. You get a screenshot of a number one spot, and the phone never moves, because no customer was looking for that phrase.
What to do
Ask which exact keywords you rank for. Then ask for evidence that homeowners actually search them, and that they match work you want to be doing.
Why it matters
That profile is where your map pin, your reviews and your calls from Google Maps come from. Google's Business Profile third party policies state that a provider must not hold a profile in exchange for payment, and that the business should keep ownership of it.
What to do
Open your profile settings and look at People and access, which shows who is an Owner and who is a Manager. If you are listed only as a manager, go to business.google.com/add, find your business, and select Request Access. Google notifies the current owner, who has three days to respond. If your business has no customer-facing address, Google Business Profile support can help instead.

Source: Google Business Profile Help, request ownership and third party policies

Why it matters
Your domain is the address of your business. Whoever controls the registrar account, meaning the account where the domain is registered, can point it somewhere else. The same goes for a website you cannot download and move: if you leave, the pages and the photos can stay behind.
What to do
Look your domain up on ICANN Lookup. Privacy services often hide the registrant name, so if it comes back masked, confirm ownership through the registrar account itself, your invoices, and your agreement. Then ask in writing: if I leave, what do I keep.
Worth knowing
In Google Search Console, open Settings and then Users and permissions. Google's documentation explains that someone shown as Owner and Verified proved ownership themselves, while Owner on its own is a delegated owner whose access the verified owner can remove. It is worth knowing which one you are.

Source: Google Search Console Help, managing owners and permissions

Why it matters
Without tracking, a good month is a story and a bad month is an excuse, and neither one can be checked. It also hides the more expensive problem: leads that came in and never got called back.
What to do
Ask how calls and form fills are traced back to the work. If your calls were recorded lawfully, with whatever notice or consent your state requires, listen to a few of them. If they were not, ask for the call logs and what happened to each lead.
Why it matters
A long term with no performance benchmark protects their revenue while promising you nothing. The part you can actually check is concrete: how much notice the agreement requires, what form the cancellation has to take, and whether it renews on its own.
What to do
Find the cancellation clause tonight and read it. Then send a plain email asking how to cancel. You are measuring how clearly and how quickly they explain it.
Why it matters
The Federal Trade Commission's final rule on fake reviews and testimonials took effect on October 21, 2024. It covers buying, selling and spreading reviews that misrepresent the reviewer or their experience, including ones produced by software, and a business can face exposure where it knew or should have known the reviews were false. Those reviews sit under your business name.
What to do
Go through the last ninety days of reviews and match each one to a real job. Anything you cannot match, ask about in writing, and keep the reply.

Source: FTC, final rule banning fake reviews and testimonials

Why it matters
Google may call to verify information on a business listing, but it will not cold-call to sell you rankings or take payment for placement. The Federal Trade Commission has taken cases like this to court. In one, a group that sold small businesses search optimization, directory listings and website services through unsolicited calls and past due invoices for things never ordered was ordered to pay more than four and a half million dollars, and permanently banned from selling those services.
What to do
Ask them to name one thing specific to roofing: storm season, insurance supplement work, or how a homeowner actually chooses a contractor. A straight answer either describes real experience or admits there is none.

Source: FTC press release, court order barring scammers marketing internet services

Part two

Add up your boxes

0 to 1
Nothing jumping out. Keep asking for booked work every month anyway. The reason it stays this way is that somebody is checking.
2 to 3
Worth a closer look. Go back through the boxes you ticked and start with anything about ownership or access, because those get harder to sort out the longer they sit.
4+
Worth sorting out properly. Start with what you own and what your agreement says, then work back through the reporting.
Read this as a rough guide. It is not a diagnosis, and the count is less important than what you found. A single ownership or access problem can matter more than several small ones. Whatever you ticked, work out what you actually own before you tell anyone you are leaving: read your agreement, confirm which assets are yours, and start any transfers through the proper channels while things are still working. If ownership turns out to be disputed, that is a conversation for a lawyer.

Part three

Five questions to ask before you pay anybody

  1. Show me one job you booked me last month, and the call it came from. They should be able to trace an example in their own reporting.
  2. Whose name is on my domain, my website, and my Google Business Profile right now? Get the answer in writing, and check it yourself as well.
  3. Which number on this report goes up when I make money? If nothing on the report connects to customers, that is worth knowing.
  4. If I leave in thirty days, what do I keep? Ask before you sign. Get the answer in writing either way.
  5. Who actually does the work, and have they worked with roofers before? Plenty of work gets passed to a team you will never meet.

Part four

What good actually looks like

  • The report opens with calls, quote requests and booked work, and the SEO detail comes after.
  • You know exactly which assets are yours, and the agreement says so in writing.
  • You get estimates and timeframes, and when they slip you get told why.
  • You can review your own call records, including the ones that went badly.
  • Cancelling is a normal email, and they explain how without being chased.
  • They can explain last month's work in language you would use with a customer.
  • When something did not work, you hear it from them before you notice it yourself.
